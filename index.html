How this works

Both models are fit by ordinary least squares on the selected period's daily readings — finding the coefficients that minimise the squared gap between predicted and actual AQI.

Linear: AQI = b₀ + b₁·temp + b₂·humidity
Polynomial: AQI = b₀ + b₁·temp + b₂·hum + b₃·temp² + b₄·hum² + b₅·temp·hum

R² is the share of AQI variation the model explains (1.0 = perfect, 0 = no better than guessing the mean). The polynomial usually fits a little better because it has more freedom to curve — but on real data that can also mean overfitting. Predictions are clamped to the valid 0–500 AQI range. Note: this is fit on representative sample weather, so treat it as illustrative of the method, not a forecast.

Health advice by AQI level

The Air Quality Index groups pollution into six bands. Here's what each means and how to protect yourself — with extra care for children, older adults, pregnant people, and anyone with heart or lung conditions.

General rule: when AQI climbs above 200, cut back on outdoor exertion. Above 300, a well-fitted N95/KN95 mask meaningfully reduces the fine particles you breathe — a cloth mask does not.

Simple ways to reduce your exposure

  • Check the AQI before outdoor exercise, and shift hard workouts to cleaner hours (often early morning).
  • Keep windows closed on high-pollution days; run an air purifier with a HEPA filter if you have one.
  • Wear a properly sealed N95/KN95 outdoors when AQI is Poor or worse.
  • Avoid busy roadsides — pollutant levels spike close to traffic.
  • Stay hydrated and don't add indoor smoke (incense, unventilated cooking, cigarettes).
This information is general and educational, not medical advice. If you have a heart or lung condition and symptoms worsen, contact a health professional.

The pollutants, and why they rise

AirAgain tracks five pollutants used in the CPCB index. Here's where each comes from and what makes it spike — especially across the Nepal and India plains and valleys during winter.

PM2.5 — fine particulate matter

Vehicle exhaust · brick kilns · crop & waste burning · winter heating

Particles under 2.5 microns are small enough to reach deep into the lungs and the bloodstream, which is why PM2.5 usually drives the AQI. It builds up when cold, still winter air traps emissions near the ground — the temperature inversions that blanket the Kathmandu Valley and the Indo-Gangetic plain (Delhi, Kanpur, Patna and beyond) from roughly November to February.

PM10 — coarse particulate matter

Road & construction dust · unpaved roads · brick kilns

Larger dust particles that irritate the airways and eyes. They rise sharply during the dry season and near construction and unpaved roads, and settle after rain washes the air.

NO₂ — nitrogen dioxide

Diesel & petrol engines · generators

A traffic-related gas that peaks during rush hours and along congested corridors. It inflames airways and, over time, contributes to the fine-particle load through chemical reactions in the atmosphere.

CO — carbon monoxide

Incomplete combustion · idling engines · indoor fires

A colourless gas from burning fuel incompletely. It rises in heavy, slow traffic and from poorly ventilated indoor stoves, and it reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen.

NH₃ — ammonia

Agriculture · fertiliser · livestock waste

Mostly agricultural. It reacts with other pollutants to form secondary fine particles, linking farm emissions to the particulate haze over populated areas.

Why winter is worst: cold air sits below warm air like a lid (a temperature inversion), trapping smoke and dust in the valley. Less rain means less natural washout, so pollution accumulates day after day until the weather shifts.

How the AQI is calculated

AirAgain uses the Indian CPCB (Central Pollution Control Board) method. Each pollutant is converted to a 0–500 sub-index; the highest sub-index becomes the overall AQI, and that pollutant is flagged as "dominant."

The six categories

The math, briefly

For a measured concentration, the index finds which band it falls in and scales linearly within that band:

Sub-index = ((I_high − I_low) / (C_high − C_low)) × (C − C_low) + I_low

The overall AQI is simply the worst (highest) of the five pollutant sub-indices, because the air is only as healthy as its most harmful component.

Pollutants & units

  • PM2.5, PM10, NO₂, NH₃ — measured in µg/m³
  • CO — measured in mg/m³
